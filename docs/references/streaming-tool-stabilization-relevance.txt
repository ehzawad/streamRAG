Tool-intent stabilization paper: relevance to the assessment
============================================================

Decision
--------
KEEP as a secondary design and evaluation reference. It is not the source of the
Stream RAG method; it analyzes when speculative retrieval from partial text can
help, when it cannot, and how to measure the difference.

Why it directly helps our typed StreamRAG choice
-----------------------------------------------
The paper explicitly analyzes input that ends when the user stops speaking or
typing. Its central condition is that "the correct query becomes determinable
before the user stops speaking or typing" (page 1).

That is exactly the uncertainty created by our interface: we will retrieve from
an evolving typed prefix before the full question is known.

What it contributes beyond the primary Stream RAG paper
-------------------------------------------------------
1. Tool-intent stabilization
   This is the point in a growing query where retrieval has found sufficient
   evidence or has become consistent with full-query retrieval. It explains why
   some questions benefit from early retrieval and others do not.

2. The late-disambiguation failure case
   If the decisive entity or constraint arrives near the end, early retrieval can
   be wrong and provide little or no latency benefit. Our system therefore needs
   final validation and fallback rather than blindly trusting the earliest call.

3. A reason to avoid per-keystroke retrieval
   Partial results can change as new words arrive. Changed-only snapshots,
   meaningful-prefix model triggering, a server-owned quiet timer, and stale-call
   cancellation control wasted work.

4. An honest explanation for mixed results
   StreamRAG is query-dependent. A small benchmark should include questions that
   stabilize early and questions whose meaning becomes clear late. StreamRAG is
   not expected to win every query, which aligns with the assessment's demand to
   explain trade-offs and failures.

How to apply it to our fixed benchmark
--------------------------------------
Construct the expected answers first, then include both kinds of queries:

Early-stabilizing examples
- The key entity and task appear near the beginning.
- A partial prefix is likely to retrieve the answer-bearing chunk.

Late-stabilizing examples
- A final entity, date, negation, comparison target, or other constraint changes
  what evidence is relevant.
- Early prefixes are deliberately ambiguous but the full question is answerable.

For every test query, replay the same word prefixes through the same retriever and
record:
- earliest prefix where the expected answer-bearing chunk enters top-k;
- whether that chunk remains available through the final prefix;
- top-k overlap with full-query retrieval;
- number of top-result changes;
- speculative calls, cancellations, reuse and final fallback;
- actual submit-to-first-token and total latency for both paths.

These are diagnostic labels and metrics. The assessment's required headline
comparison remains speed, cost, accuracy and overall performance on the identical
fixed test set.

Implementation decisions it supports
------------------------------------
- Use bounded meaningful-prefix eligibility and a zero-shot structured model
  decision for evolving changed drafts, not a trained trigger.
- After one delivered draft remains unchanged for 500 ms, start deterministic
  retrieval with that exact text.
- Keep at most one active speculative retrieval and cancel stale work after a
  correction or replacement query.
- At Send, accept only completed or in-flight retrieval whose recorded source text
  literally equals the committed text.
- Use bounded exact committed-text retrieval for any mismatch, change, or failed
  exact in-flight retrieval.
- Generate one final grounded response after Send; never expose a provisional
  answer while the user is typing.

What not to import or claim
---------------------------
- Do not reproduce the paper's full CRAG prefix-sweep study.
- Do not use its reported 73.9% streamable fraction as our expected result.
- Do not treat top-result stability as proof that the result is correct; a wrong
  document can remain stably wrong.
- Do not claim its aggregate latency bound predicts individual queries.
- Do not generalize its single English QA benchmark to our corpus without testing.
- Do not add a learned trigger unless our own evidence justifies the complexity.

Evidence-strength caveat
------------------------
This is a recent arXiv paper and should be cited as supporting analysis, not as the
assignment's definition of StreamRAG. Its strongest value for us is experimental
design: it prevents a cherry-picked benchmark and gives names to early stability,
late disambiguation, speculative misfires and wasted calls.

Observed development check
--------------------------
The retained five-question development run preserved the automatic answer/support/
citation proxies on both paths and showed mixed early/late latency behavior. Exact
metrics live only in the canonical benchmark report and generated summary so this
interpretation note cannot become a second stale result sheet. The small run does
not prove the labels or a causal or universal speedup. It is not a test-set
conclusion; all 10 unseen queries remain sealed behind human approval.

Relationship among the three documents
--------------------------------------
Assessment: defines what must be built and measured.
Stream RAG paper: defines the primary Path B mechanism.
Stabilization paper: explains when that mechanism helps and how to test it fairly.

Source
------
When Does Streaming Tool Use Help? Characterizing Tool-Intent Stabilization in
Streaming Retrieval-Augmented Generation, Elroy Galbraith, arXiv:2606.20113v2.
Relevant pages: 1-8, especially Sections 3, 5.1, 5.3-5.5, and the limitations.
